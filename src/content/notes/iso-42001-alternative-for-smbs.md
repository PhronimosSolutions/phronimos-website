ISO 42001 is the international standard for AI management systems, and the substance is right: risk assessment, documented controls, ongoing monitoring, accountability. The certification process, for a 25-person firm, is not right — it costs $37,500–$85,000 in year one and roughly $115,000 over three years, and most of that spend goes to auditor fees and process overhead rather than the underlying governance discipline. What a small business actually needs is ISO-42001-shaped governance at SMB scale, without the certification.

## What ISO 42001 actually requires

ISO 42001 defines an AI management system, or AIMS. The substance breaks into four working parts:

- **A risk assessment.** For every AI system in scope, an evaluation of what can go wrong, how likely it is, and what the impact would be. Updated on a defined cadence and after material changes.
- **Documented controls.** The specific policies, procedures, and technical mechanisms the firm uses to manage each identified risk. Written down. Reviewed.
- **Ongoing monitoring and measurement.** Evidence that the controls are actually operating — not just that they exist on paper. Metrics, logs, incident records.
- **Management review and continuous improvement.** A recurring formal cycle in which leadership reviews the AIMS, decides what to change, and documents the decision.

For a firm of any size operating AI in production, all four of those are the right things to do. A 25-person insurance agency running AI in the quote pipeline should have a risk assessment, documented controls, monitoring evidence, and a review cadence. The disagreement isn't with the discipline. It's with the paperwork surrounding the certificate.

## What the certification costs, and what the cost buys

The $37,500–$85,000 year-one range covers, roughly: gap analysis by a consultancy ($10,000–$25,000), internal preparation time (often 200–400 person-hours, valued conservatively at $15,000–$40,000), the certification audit itself by an accredited registrar ($12,500–$20,000), and remediation of any nonconformities the audit finds. The ~$115,000 over three years adds surveillance audits (annual, ~$6,000–$12,000 each) and a re-certification audit in year three.

What that spend buys is the *certificate*. The governance work — the risk assessment, the controls, the monitoring — is what the firm builds during preparation. The certificate is a third-party attestation that the firm built it to the standard's specification.

For a large enterprise, or for a firm selling AI-heavy services to enterprises that require the certificate as a procurement gate, the attestation is worth what it costs. For a 25-person firm serving other small businesses, the attestation is worth very little and the money would be better spent on the underlying controls.

## Which parts a small firm actually needs

Almost all of the substance. Specifically:

- **The risk assessment.** Yes. A written document, reviewed annually and after any material change to an AI system in production. This is the input to almost every other decision.
- **The documented controls.** Yes. Written policies for evaluation, deployment, monitoring, incident response, vendor diligence, and record retention. These are also what a NAIC examiner, an E&O carrier, or a carrier partner will ask to see. See [NAIC AI governance for a small insurance agency](/notes/naic-ai-governance-for-smb-insurance/).
- **The monitoring evidence.** Yes. Actual alerts on actual production systems, not intentions. The [reliability standard](/reliability/) is the operational form of this.
- **A review cadence.** Yes. A quarterly review is realistic for a 5–50 person firm.

## Which parts of the certification process are dead weight for a small firm

- **Formal management review meetings on the ISO 42001 cadence.** A firm with a five-person leadership team already reviews the business quarterly. A separate formal meeting with a documented agenda that mirrors the ISO clause structure is process overhead the size of the firm doesn't support.
- **Auditor fees.** For a firm not being asked for the certificate by a buyer, the auditor is paid to produce an artifact nobody is asking to see.
- **The gap analysis consultancy phase.** For a firm with an existing operator on the AI workflows, the gaps are already visible.
- **The formal nonconformity remediation and re-audit cycle.** Useful when the certificate is the goal. Not useful when the goal is the underlying discipline.

## The honest framing

ISO 42001 is a well-designed standard, and the standard document itself is worth reading if a firm's leadership takes AI governance seriously. Certifying against it, at 25 people, is a category error — spending enterprise money on enterprise process to produce an enterprise artifact when what the firm needs is the underlying operational discipline. The alternative is not "skip governance." It is: build the same controls the standard requires, at the scale the firm can actually operate, and document them well enough to satisfy a regulator, a carrier, or a serious buyer without the auditor in the middle.
